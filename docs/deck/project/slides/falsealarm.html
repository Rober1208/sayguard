This is where we applied the Jev idea first. Before an alert reaches the officer, a violation has to persist, repeats count once, and then a double-check looks only at the crop and answers one question: hard hat, yes or no. Our first double-check asked a two-billion-parameter generative vision-language model, Qwen2-VL-2B. Jev itself cannot run on site, so we built a model in its style: a frozen CLIP image encoder and a small trained decision head that only answers yes or no. On the PC it is about eighteen times faster, and it is also more accurate on the same held-out crops: it clears more crops of people who do wear a hard hat, our stand-in for wrong flags, and confirms slightly more real violations. On the Hailo-10H the difference is larger still: by Hailo's own figures the CLIP encoder runs at 81.5 frames per second, while Qwen2-VL-2B needs more than a second per crop for its first token alone. The encoder is already in the Hailo Model Zoo. Quantized to INT8, it keeps the same accuracy in Hailo's emulator once the head is retrained on INT8 features; compiling it for the chip is our week-two task in Stage II. Filtered alerts are never deleted; they stay in the feed, dimmed, for review.
